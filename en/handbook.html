THE FRAME

Read the Parashah Yourself

What it comes down to

The text is a land. Every interpretation is a map. A good map helps us see the land, but it must never invent the land. And this land is special: here God speaks to us.

Based on The Frame method of Arie Alberts

Beforehand

This booklet wants to teach us something that sounds simple but takes a lifetime of practice: to listen before we interpret. A parashah is not a pantry from which we quickly take a beautiful thought. It is a portion of God’s Word into which we may enter, where we may stand and allow ourselves to be addressed.

We do not need to have studied theology. We need attention. Patience. A willingness to leave a question open for a while. And above all: a heart that does not only want to understand, but also wants to receive.

One simple discipline therefore runs through this booklet: first listen to what is there, then interpret, and keep testing our interpretation against the text.

Two ways to use this booklet

We can use this booklet in two ways. Sometimes we investigate a parashah ourselves and build a study, reading or conversation from it. Then The Frame helps us first to see what is there, then to distinguish what we believe we hear in it, and finally to build something we can inhabit together.

At other times we hear or read someone else’s parashah teaching. Then too, we are not called to accept everything without thinking. But neither are we called to listen with folded arms, searching for what is wrong. In His Word, God teaches us to test precisely because He wants us to receive what is good.

Paul writes: “Test all things; hold fast what is good” (1 Thessalonians 5:21). When people speak in the congregation, he also says that the others may evaluate and discern what is said (1 Corinthians 14:29). And to Timothy he writes that all Scripture is inspired by God and useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting and training in righteousness, so that the person of God may be equipped for every good work (2 Timothy 3:16–17).

Testing therefore does not stand opposite listening. It belongs to listening. We do not test in order to place ourselves above another person, but so that together we remain under the Word. We do not grasp at the right to judge someone. We want to receive what God teaches us to discern through Scripture.

We do not test the person.

We do not know the speaker’s heart. We do not always know the road someone has walked with God, the struggle behind an insight or the grace received in it. When someone shares something with us, we may first stand beside that person. We listen with them. We try to understand what they have seen and what they want to pass on to us.

What we do weigh are the words, the connections and the place a statement is given. Does the text truly say this? Is it a reliable interpretation of what is written? Is it a confession carried by the whole of Scripture? Or is it a personal application: a beautiful thought that helped someone and may also build us up?

A beautiful application does not have to be the direct meaning of the text in order to be instructive and edifying.

That is precisely why we do not have to place everything in the same category. An application can be precious without our saying, “This is what this text means.” An image can touch us without becoming proof. An experience can encourage us without prescribing the same road for everyone. By preserving these distinctions, we reject nothing. We give every word the place where it can genuinely bear fruit.

In this way, weighing does not become a cold assessment, but a form of learning together. We receive what is good. We investigate what remains open. We let go of what the text cannot bear. And we keep the person in love, even when we weigh an interpretation differently.

The goal is not that after every teaching we know exactly who is right. The goal is that Scripture teaches, corrects and builds us up; that we learn to understand one another; and that together we remain close to Yeshua. He is not only the subject of our interpretation. He is the living Word in whom we abide while we listen, discern and retain what is good.

This is how we begin

Those who want to start immediately can take the five questions from chapter 3 and then follow the forty-five-minute route in chapter 17. The worksheet at the back helps make our thoughts visible. Not because a worksheet listens in our place, but because paper sometimes gives us enough calm not to jump too quickly.

“Speak, for Your servant is listening.”

1 Samuel 3:10

That is the posture with which we begin. Not: Lord, confirm what I have already devised. But: speak, and teach us to hear. The method is not a ladder by which we climb up to God. At most, it is a way of opening our hands to what He has already given.

One boundary never to forget

What we learn from a text and apply to our lives can be precious and true, but it is not automatically the same as what the text literally says. An application becomes unreliable as soon as we push it back into the text and act as though God spoke precisely our application. We receive application from the Word, but we do not turn it into the Word.

PART I — THE GROUND

—

1. Map and land

Apply this:To the step-by-step plan

When we read a text from Scripture, we never enter completely empty-handed. We bring our questions. Our upbringing. What we have heard before. The congregation in which we were formed. Perhaps also our disappointments, our fears and the answers for which we long.

We all look through a particular lens.

That is not wrong in itself. Without a lens, we often see nothing at all. The danger arises when we forget that we are wearing a lens. Then we no longer think, this is how I see the text, but, this is how the text is. Our way of seeing then quietly becomes equal to God’s own speech.

That is why The Frame uses a simple image:

The text is the land. Our interpretation is the map.

A map can be very valuable. When we walk through unfamiliar territory, a map helps us see the roads. It shows where a river runs, where a mountain stands and which route we can take. But the map does not make that river. It does not put the mountain in place. It can only try to represent as honestly as possible what is already there.

And when the map is wrong, the land does not change.

If we have drawn a bridge on our map but find ourselves before a wide river with no crossing, it is pointless to tell the land to adapt to our map. The map must be corrected.

So it is when we read Scripture.

We can make diagrams. We can discover connections. We can examine words, draw lines and formulate applications. That is good. It helps us see. But our interpretation does not create the truth. It must repeatedly bow to what God has actually spoken.

That is the first distinction we must learn to preserve.

God speaks — and what He speaks has creative power.

“And God said, ‘Let there be light,’ and there was light.”

Genesis 1:3

We speak about what God has spoken. That is something different. Only the Word of God brings reality into being. Our words may bear witness. They may point. They may open what we had not yet seen. But they must never take the place of the Word itself.

John says of Yeshua:

“All things were made through the Word, and without Him nothing was made that has been made.”

John 1:3

That is a boundary that makes us small, yet at the same time gives us rest.

We do not have to make the text powerful. We do not have to help God speak. We do not have to find a hidden system that finally causes something extraordinary to emerge from Scripture. God has already spoken. The Word is already living. We do not need to kindle the light; we may open our eyes to the light that is already there.

Yet this is precisely where our temptation lies.

We can find an interpretation so beautiful that we no longer want to release it. We have discovered something, seen a pattern or made a connection, and it feels as though everything suddenly falls into place. Then something can happen in us. We no longer receive the discovery as a gift that may still be tested. We begin to hold on to it. We want to secure it.

We begin to grasp.

The difference is sometimes small and hard to see. Receiving says:

“Lord, show me what You have spoken. And when I am looking wrongly, correct me.”

Grasping says:

“I now know how it is. The text will have to confirm my interpretation.”

In receiving, the hand stays open. In grasping, the hand closes.

Think of a child who receives something beautiful. As long as the child receives the gift, there remains a relationship with the giver. The child looks up. Wonders. Perhaps says thank you. But as soon as the child clutches the gift and becomes afraid that someone will take it away, something changes. The gift becomes property. What was intended to bring joy becomes something that must be defended.

The same can happen with an interpretation.

Even a good interpretation can be held in the wrong way. The problem is then not always what we say, but how we hold it. Does it remain a received insight under the authority of God’s Word? Or have we made it into something that gives us certainty, influence or spiritual status?

Therefore we do not ask only:

Is this interpretation beautiful?

We also ask:

Can this interpretation still be corrected by the text?

That is not weak faith. It is a form of trust. We do not have to defend our interpretation anxiously, because truth does not depend on our interpretation. The text remains where it is, even when our interpretation must be adjusted.

“Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path.”

Psalm 119:105

Notice: the Word is the light. Not our method. Not our diagram. Not our cleverness. The Frame can help us look, but it cannot produce the light. A lamp in our hand is not the same as the path on which we walk. And even with a good lamp, we must keep looking where we put our feet.

That requires humility.

But humility does not mean that we may say nothing. Nor does it mean that every interpretation is equally uncertain. We may investigate. We may learn. We may speak with conviction when the text is clear. But we continue to speak as people who themselves stand under the Word.

We do not stand above the text with our interpretation in hand.

We walk within it.

We ourselves are addressed. Corrected. Comforted. Exposed. Scripture is not an object we examine from a distance while we remain out of view. God addresses us in the very land where we think we are merely drawing a route for others.

Perhaps that is the deepest test of every interpretation: does it bring us closer to control, or closer to obedience? Does it make us greater, or more receptive? Does it give us a system with which we can explain everything, or teach us to remain with the One who spoke the Word?

“Abide in Me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit by itself unless it abides in the vine, neither can you unless you abide in Me.”

John 15:4

That is also where our engagement with Scripture ends.

Not with a perfect map.

Not with the thought that we now understand everything.

Not with a method that makes us independent.

We end with abiding.

We receive the Word from God’s hand. We examine it carefully. We draw a map that can help others walk with us. And where our map is wrong, we allow it to be corrected. Not because truth is uncertain, but because we do not possess it.

The truth carries us.

We do not carry it.

And that is precisely where grace lies: we do not have to make the road ourselves, but may walk in Him who said:

“I am the Way, the Truth and the Life.”

John 14:6

The great pitfall

Confusing our interpretation with God’s own speech. This happens when the text is no longer allowed to correct our interpretation, but only has to prove that we are right. Then we no longer receive what God speaks, but grasp for a certainty we can control. A good interpretation remains open to correction and points away from itself. It does not help us become masters over the Word, but remain disciples in Yeshua.

Why the text speaks

Beneath everything we do in this booklet lies one confession: Scripture does not speak only because it is ancient, wise or impressive. It speaks with authority because God makes Himself known through this Word. Human beings wrote in their own language, history and character, but they were carried by the Spirit of God.

“All Scripture is inspired by God and is useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting and training.”

2 Timothy 3:16

“People spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit.”

2 Peter 1:21

That is precisely why we do not need to dig beneath the text as though God’s real voice were hidden somewhere behind the words. The words are not a shell we must break open in order to reach God. God humbled Himself into human language so that He could truly address us. He comes near.

That does not mean every sentence is easy. Some passages require study. We must learn historical circumstances, compare translations and sometimes admit that we do not yet know something. But difficult is different from secret. Scripture asks us to listen, not to find a key that grants access only to a few.

E. J. Young expressed this confession clearly. He held two things together: Scripture truly is God’s Word, and it was truly written by human beings. The writer’s voice is not erased; it is carried. This preserves us both from unbelief and from a mechanical way of handling the text.

We may therefore read with expectation. The same Spirit who carried the writers also opens our understanding now. Not so that we rise above the text, but so that we hear what God has given and come to know Yeshua in it.

“Then He opened their minds so that they could understand the Scriptures.”

Luke 24:45

So we do not read in order to grasp hidden power. We read because God has spoken. We receive the Word, keep it, respond to it and abide in Him to whom all these words bear witness.

2. Five layers, in the right order

Apply this:To the step-by-step plan

When we prepare a meal, we do not throw everything into one pot at once and hope the flavours will remain recognisable. We cut, weigh and add ingredients at the right time. Not because the ingredients have nothing to do with one another, but precisely because they come together well only when we first learn to distinguish them.

So it is with reading Scripture. What is written, what we feel about it, what we confess about God and what it asks of us ultimately belong together. But when we mix them from the first moment, we later no longer know what came from the text and what came from ourselves.

That is why we work with five layers. Not as five rooms in which we confine God, but as five moments of attention.

Layer The question It goes wrong when…
1. What does the text say? Words, actions, repetitions, contrasts and structure. we already write down our interpretation as if it were an observation.
2. How is it experienced? Desire, fear, hope, shame, wonder and imagery. we immediately turn our experience into evidence.
3. What is revealed about God and humanity? Source, calling, covenant, sin, grace and destination. we go further than the ordinary meaning can bear.
4. What do we confess? Its coherence with the whole of Scripture and with Yeshua. we present our confession of faith as a neutral observation.
5. What does it ask of us? Response, obedience, rhythm, community and daily practice. the application becomes larger than the Word that carries it.

The first layer asks: what actually lies before us? Who speaks? To whom? What happens first and what follows? Which words return? This is the work of our eyes and ears. We are not yet trying to say what it “means for us.” We look.

The second layer asks about the world of the text. How must it have sounded to Israel, standing at the border of the land, to hear the wilderness journey again? What does Zion experience when she says that God has forgotten her? Experience is not proof, but it does help us understand that the Word came to real people.

The third layer asks what becomes visible about God, humanity and the movement of His Kingdom. Here we begin to interpret. We ask about source, direction and fruit. But here too we remain close to what is actually written.

The fourth layer is confession. We read as people who believe that all Scripture coheres and finds its centre in Yeshua. We do not hide that faith. At the same time, we do not pretend that every connection can be derived directly from one isolated sentence. We say honestly: this is what we confess, and this is how we understand the coherence.

The fifth layer is application. The Word does not only want to be observed; it wants to be heard and done. But obedience is a response. It is not the source from which the Word receives its authority. First God speaks. Then we answer.

“Be doers of the Word, and not hearers only.”

James 1:22

This text too begins with hearing. We can respond only to what we have received. When we reverse the order, application becomes an assignment we squeeze out of the text ourselves. Then we grasp a lesson before we have allowed the Word to finish speaking.

Why the order is fixed

Even an interpretation that is true in itself can have been reached wrongly when we place it on the text before looking. The fact that we eventually say something true does not make the reversed route reliable. God gives; we hear; we distinguish; we confess; we respond. The order preserves the gift.

The layers are therefore not an end in themselves. They help us not to move too quickly. At the end they may come together: mind, heart, faith and life. But they come together as received parts, not as ingredients we have forcefully kneaded into one message.

And when we notice that we have moved too quickly, we do not have to become trapped in shame. We can return. We can read again. Grace also means that our map may be redrawn while we continue walking in Yeshua.

3. Five questions for every text

Apply this:Step 4

Sometimes a method can seem so extensive that we do not know where to begin. Then five simple questions help. We can ask them of almost every text. They are not a trick that always produces the same answer. They are open hands with which we receive what this passage actually gives.

  1. What is given, said or done here?

  2. How do the people in the text itself view what is happening?

  3. Where do the authority, strength or gift come from?

  4. What movement do we see: from where to where?

  5. Is something received and answered, or grasped and controlled?

1. What is given, said or done here?

This question keeps us with what is concrete. A promise is spoken. A command is given. Someone departs. A people remembers. Yeshua answers. By first collecting verbs, we often see more than when we immediately search for a theme.

2. How do the people themselves experience it?

A story has an inner world. People are afraid, hope, complain, trust or do not yet understand. We do not have to approve of their experience in order to take it seriously. Those who skip the fear sometimes also fail to understand the temptation to grasp.

3. Where does it come from?

This is a question of source. Does the word come from God? Is the strength a gift? Is something driven by fear, habit or rivalry? The outside may look the same while the source differs. Two people may obey; one responds out of love, while the other tries through obedience to secure a place.

“Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above.”

James 1:17

4. What movement is within it?

Scripture rarely stands still. God calls from slavery to freedom, from exile to return, from death to life. Sometimes we also see the reverse movement: from received abundance to forgetting, from wonder to control, from brotherhood to rivalry. When we see the movement, we often understand better what an individual word is doing.

5. Receiving or grasping?

This question exposes the heart. Not only: what does someone do? But: how is the good held? Does it remain a gift, or become a possession? Is God’s word answered, or used to force a desired outcome?

Take Deuteronomy 8. Israel receives manna, land and strength. The danger appears not only in hunger, but precisely when the storehouses become full. Then a person can say: my strength made this. The same hands that first received bread begin to behave as though they are the source.

These five questions slow us down. They prevent us from moving straight from one striking word to a spiritual conclusion. And they keep application close: God gives, we hear, we respond.

When we cannot answer everything, that is not immediately a failure. An open question can be more honest than a quick answer. We may remain with what has been given. We do not have to fill every open question today. Yeshua asks us to abide in Him and walk in the light we have received.

PART II — FIRST LOOK CAREFULLY

—

4. Marking off and dividing the text

Apply this:Step 1Step 3

Anyone who wants to lay out a garden does not begin with one flower that catches the eye. First one looks at the boundaries, where the sun falls, how the water drains and what already grows. Only then does one decide where something can be planted. So we also begin with a parashah: first the whole, then the thread.

We record the fixed details. Which parashah are we reading? Which haftarah belongs with it? Is it an ordinary Shabbat, a feast season, a period of mourning or comfort? For whom are we preparing something: our family, a study group, a congregation or only ourselves?

Those questions seem practical, but they protect the meaning. The same words can resonate differently at another point in the year. Not because the text changes, but because the community stands at another place in its shared walk.

Then we divide the text into natural sections. We watch for a different speaker, a new place, a jump in time, a repeated command, a contrast or a concluding sentence. Each section receives a short title describing what we see.

Good — and too early

Good: “Moses reminds Israel of the journey through the wilderness.” Too early: “God teaches that all our suffering is necessary.” The first sentence describes the section. The second already turns one particular history into a general doctrine.

A good descriptive title can sometimes sound almost dull. That is all right. We are not preaching yet. We are placing markers in the land so that later we know where we are walking.

“They read from the book, from the Law of God, gave the meaning and explained it so that the people understood.”

Nehemiah 8:8

Reading, explaining and understanding belong together, but they are not the same. Those who skip reading detach the interpretation from its source. Those who skip explanation may hear words without seeing their coherence. The Frame seeks to preserve that movement.

Sometimes we later discover that our division was not right. A sentence we took as a conclusion turns out to be the opening of the next section. Then too we adjust the map. We do not have to defend our first division. The text may correct us.

In this way, marking boundaries is not a form of control, but an exercise in received attention. We make room. We say, as it were: this is the piece of land given to us today; let us not run across it, but walk in it together.

5. First see what is there

Apply this:Step 2Step 4

The first meaning of a text is often called the peshat: what stands in the ordinary language, sentence and context. Ordinary does not mean shallow. A river can be deep and still remain a real river. Depth does not arise because we leave the ordinary behind, but because we descend into it honestly.

Yeshua once asked an expert in the Law, “What is written in the Law? How do you read it?” Those two questions belong together. What is written? And how do we read? The first brings us to the text. The second makes us aware of how we read.

“What is written in the Law? How do you read it?”

Luke 10:26

For each section we therefore write down: who speaks, and to whom? What is promised, forbidden, done or remembered? Which words return? What cause and effect are named? Where does the direction change?

We also notice what the text does not say. Silence is not permission to fill things in ourselves. When Scripture does not tell us why someone did something, we must be careful with motives. A possibility can help, but we give it a different label from an established fact.

A simple exercise is to make two columns. On the left we write only observable details. On the right we put our first thoughts and questions. This lets us see when an interpretation has disguised itself as a fact.

For example: “Israel was hungry” is an observation. “God wanted to break Israel until it could do nothing” is already an interpretation. Perhaps that thought touches something, perhaps not. But it may be weighed only later, and then it must remain connected to the words of the passage.

First seeing requires self-control. We often want to move quickly to the one insight we can share. But unripe fruit tastes astringent. The person who picks it too early has something in hand, but misses the fullness that time and sunlight could have given.

Therefore we remain with the text a little longer. We do not have to force the Word to produce something new today. Sometimes grace is precisely that we see again what was already there. Yeshua is not less alive when our interpretation becomes simpler. Often He comes nearer precisely when we stop trying to confine Him within our discovery.

6. Words — and secret numbers and letters?

Apply this:Step 5

Words matter. One verb can show who acts. A repeated word can form the backbone of a passage. Therefore we study words carefully. But here too the rule applies: a tool helps only when we use it for its intended purpose.

When we study a Hebrew word, we first look at its form in this sentence. Then at its use in comparable passages, its grammatical context and fair translation possibilities. The ancient origin of a word may be interesting, but it does not automatically decide what the word means here.

Be careful with word roots

A root is not a remote control that gives every form of a word the same meaning. The sentence, form and usage all help determine what is actually being said. An ancient association may shed light, but it may not overwrite ordinary language.

Paleo-Hebrew, letter images and gematria

We regularly encounter interpretations in which a Hebrew word is said to contain a hidden message because each letter once arose from a small picture. Aleph was associated with an ox, bet with a house and mem with water. The fact that letters have a pictorial history is different from saying that we can construct the meaning of every word from those pictures.

In the biblical text, letters function first of all as written signs for sounds and words. When we turn every ancient picture into an independent meaning, we can build almost any message we want. It then looks as though we are receiving something, but in reality we are laying our own meaning over the text.

Gematria works differently. Here letters also have numerical values, and words or names are connected through their number. This is a real and ancient reading practice. Yet the same boundary applies: a number must never carry the foundation of a doctrine that cannot be found in the text without that number.

The one question that decides much

Does the number or letter image carry the conclusion, or does it merely show something beautiful that the text already says? If it carries the conclusion, we must go back. If it adorns a meaning already firmly present in the text, it may be mentioned as a possibility.

We can compare it with stained glass. Coloured glass can make the light beautifully visible. But if there is no light behind the window, the glass does not create the sun. Letter images and numbers can sometimes add colour; they do not bring forth the Word.

When can a number carry meaning? When the text itself explicitly uses the number. Seven days, twelve tribes, forty years: sometimes the number visibly shapes the structure. Matthew himself counts the generations in groups. Revelation even invites the reader in one place to calculate a number. Then our attention begins with what the writer does, not with our desire for a hidden code.

We may also honestly describe how other readers or ancient interpreters connected a word. Then we say: this is how it has been read. We do not automatically turn a historical reading into the only meaning of God’s speech.

So-called Bible codes go further astray. By skipping letters or searching for patterns, one can find almost anything. A method that can never say “no” can prove nothing. It locks itself within its own correctness.

Three questions when we encounter something unusual

  • Does the conclusion remain true when we remove the number or letter image?

  • Does the text itself play with this number or form, or are we putting it in?

  • Are we using it as decoration, or do we need it as a building block?

We do not need to fear that without a secret layer we will miss something of God. The Word became flesh. God did not hide Himself in a calculation, but came to dwell among us in Yeshua.

“The Word became flesh and dwelt among us.”

John 1:14

There lies our rest. We may investigate, compare and wonder. But we remain with the given Word. We do not grasp for hidden power. We receive the Shepherd’s voice and learn to follow Him.

7. Torah, haftarah and a reading from the Gospel

Apply this:Step 5Step 6Step 7Step 11

With a parashah we often place three readings beside one another: the Torah, the haftarah from the Prophets and sometimes a passage from the Gospel or the letters. This can feel as though we open three windows in the same house. Different light enters through each window, but we must keep looking at what is actually outside.

The connection does not arise because we find three isolated words that happen to resemble each other. It arises when the same movement becomes visible through the passages. Perhaps it concerns remembering and forgetting. Given bread and human strength. Exile and return. Sonship received and not used as a means of self-preservation.

Therefore we first examine each reading on its own. We give the Torah room to speak. Then the haftarah. Only after that do we place the maps beside one another. Otherwise we risk already using the prophet or the Gospel to make the first text say what we need.

Next we consider two or three possible connecting threads. Not one immediately. The first connection that moves us is not always the connection that explains the greatest amount of the texts. We ask: which thread leaves the words most intact? Which connection needs the least force? What remains open with each possibility?

“Beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, He explained to them what was written about Him in all the Scriptures.”

Luke 24:27

Yeshua teaches us that Moses and the Prophets move toward Him. But He does not teach us that every detail without distinction is a hidden picture of Him. He opens the Scriptures; He does not erase their own history.

Therefore we also give a proposed Gospel reading an honest label. We can say: this reading helps us see the movement in Yeshua. That is different from claiming that the original parashah was written only to make precisely this one connection.

When the thread fits, something beautiful happens. The Torah is not made smaller, the prophet does not become an appendix and the Gospel does not become a detached Christian layer. We hear one God speaking faithfully through history. We are carried from promise to fulfilment, from gift to response and from the Word on paper to life in Yeshua.

And when we find no convincing connection, we do not have to force one. That too is receiving. We may leave three readings beside one another and wait. The Spirit does not depend on our discovery. He can show us later what we did not yet see today.

PART III — TOOLS FOR WEIGHING

—

8. Wait to interpret until you have looked carefully

Apply this:To the step-by-step plan

We live in a time when speed is often mistaken for insight. The person who can immediately say something appears sharp. But in Scripture, waiting is not empty. Mary treasured words in her heart. The disciples understood some things only later. A seed we repeatedly pull from the ground to see whether it is growing never gets the chance to take root.

“Mary treasured all these words and pondered them in her heart.”

Luke 2:19

That is why we do not immediately apply the four questions from the next chapter. First we must have seen enough to weigh something genuinely. For every important section, we must be able to say: this is what it says; this is a possible interpretation; this remains open.

Waiting does not mean becoming passive. We read again. We compare translations. We place repetitions beside one another. We ask questions without immediately closing them. This is not a lack of faith, but reverence for the fact that we receive what God has spoken.

Interpreting too early can sometimes look spiritual. We want to find the core, point to Yeshua and name the application. But when the fruit is still green, even a good conclusion can become hard. Then we use the text to say something we already wanted to say.

The moment for interpretation will come. The Frame does not remain with description alone. But the hinge must be in the right place. First we separate what threatens to become mixed. Then we fill. First we open the hand. Then we receive what is placed in it.

Those who learn to wait discover that Scripture itself often provides more coherence than we could construct. Then the interpretation does not become weaker, but calmer. It needs to prove less. It can abide in the Word and ultimately end with the grace of Yeshua, not with the speed of our own discovery.

9. Four questions for every text

Apply this:Step 7Step 9Step 10Step 14

Now that we have looked, we may begin to weigh. Together the four questions form a sieve. Not to grind the living Word into small pieces, but to distinguish sand from stone. The pitfall often lies precisely in the question we would rather skip.

Above all four stands one foundational question: are we confusing our interpretation with what the text itself says?

Question What do we look for?
Who is speaking, and where does it come from? Does the direction come from God, or from fear, power, habit, rivalry or reaction? Is strength received as a gift, or is the means turned into the source?
Where does this come from and where is it going? What earlier event continues to work here? Is there a path, a reversal or a fulfilment? Or does the interpretation merely circle around its own correctness?
What is given, and what is asked? Does the gift come first and is obedience a response? Or must a person perform first in order to receive?
Is it truly coherent, or merely self-consistent? Does the interpretation fit the words, the context, the whole of Scripture and life? Can the text still contradict it?

Who is speaking, and where does it come from?

Not every forceful word has the same source. A person may say something that sounds devout out of fear. A habit may become so familiar that we mistake it for God’s voice. Therefore we ask not only what is being said, but also where the movement comes from.

“Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above.”

James 1:17

When something truly is a gift, the Giver remains visible. As soon as our skill, knowledge or obedience makes itself the source, grasping begins.

Where does it come from and where is it going?

A text stands within a history. The exodus echoes in the wilderness. Exile echoes in words of comfort. The cross echoes in the call to give ourselves. We therefore look for trajectory: what is continued, reversed or brought to fulfilment?

“As rain and snow come down and do not return without watering the earth, so will My word be: it will accomplish what pleases Me.”

Isaiah 55:10–11

God’s Word moves somewhere. An interpretation that only circles around our own position often misses that living direction.

What is given, and what is asked?

Scripture knows genuine obedience. It calls, commands and forms. But God’s command comes within a covenant He initiates. Israel is led out of Egypt before learning to walk at Sinai. We are not placed in Yeshua by good works; we are created in Him to walk in good works.

“I urge you, therefore, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies to God.”

Romans 12:1

Notice the order: by the mercies of God. The call is real, but it grows from received grace.

Is it truly coherent, or merely self-consistent?

Some explanations are beautifully closed within themselves. Every objection becomes new proof. Failure proves that we had too little faith; success proves that the system works. In that way, nothing can say “no” any longer.

A healthy interpretation dares to name what could correct it. It puts its cards on the table. It lets context, other texts and reality speak. That is not surrendering to doubt, but trusting that truth does not need to be protected by closed reasoning.

“They examined the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so.”

Acts 17:11

The hidden mirror error

Sometimes we do not build our interpretation from the text, but from resistance to someone else. We reject ecclesiastical authority and then make our own feeling untouchable. We oppose legalism and begin to speak as though obedience no longer matters. Then we become the mirror image of what we reject.1

Therefore we ask: what is this interpretation reacting against? And is the alternative truly received from Scripture, or merely the other side of the same movement?

The four questions are not intended to make us suspicious of every interpretation. They are intended to free us from the need to defend something we have built ourselves. We may weigh, correct and begin again. The Giver remains faithful even when our interpretation must change. We abide in Yeshua, who Himself is the truth.

10. Two labels: how certain is it, and what may it do?

Apply this:Step 8Step 11Step 16

When we store jars in a shed, we label them. Not because the contents change, but so that later we do not accidentally use salt where sugar is needed. In the same way, we give our building blocks a label.

We need two labels. The first says how certain something is. The second says what we may do with it. Those two are not the same. A historical fact may be well established and yet not prove a spiritual conclusion. A confession of faith may carry our whole life, while we honestly say that it is not scientifically derived from one isolated verse.

Label: how certain? Meaning
Confession We believe this within the coherence of Scripture and faith in Yeshua. It is not the same as a neutral research fact.
Established This can be clearly verified from the words, grammar, history or reliable research.
Possible This may help as an image, connection or deeper perspective, but we do not build a necessary conclusion on it.
Uncertain / wrong There is too little support, or the interpretation goes beyond what the text can bear. We let it go or clearly name the boundary.
Label: what may it do? Use
Carry A main conclusion or central movement can genuinely rest on this.
Support It confirms something already carried by stronger evidence.
Illustrate It makes something visible or tangible, but proves nothing.
Do not use It is too weak, too speculative or methodologically mistaken to function within the interpretation.

This double label frees us from two extremes. We do not have to pretend that everything is uncertain. Nor do we have to fix every beautiful possibility as truth. We can say: this is established; this we confess; this is a useful image; this we do not yet know.

This is also pastoral. When we present a possibility as an absolute message, we sometimes place a burden on people that God has not placed on them. An image may help someone, but it must not bind the conscience as though it were a direct command.

With every important sentence in our study, we can therefore place two small questions in the margin: how certain is this? And what am I allowing it to do?

Sometimes we discover that a weak building block was carrying our whole house. That can be frightening. But it is grace when we see it in time. Yeshua does not ask us to keep our construction standing. He invites us to build on Him.

“No one can lay any other foundation than the one already laid, which is Yeshua the Messiah.”

1 Corinthians 3:11

PART IV — THE MOVEMENT

—

11. The seven stages and the feasts

Apply this:Step 12

A study is not only a collection of correct observations. It moves. We begin in rest, separate what has become mixed, interpret, hear a call, practise, begin to dwell and test the fruit. We place that movement beside the rhythm of Israel’s feasts.

The feasts do not create the meaning. They are not a mould into which we must press every text. The text determines what is said. The feasts help us order the movement: liberation, cleansing, gift, awakening, reconciliation, dwelling and rest. Along that path we also recognise how God’s action reaches fulfilment in Yeshua.2

Time / stage Feast What we do
Beforehand Weekly Shabbat We begin in received rest. Our performance does not open the Word; God invites us.
1 Passover Looking and separating: gathering what is written without fixing its meaning too soon.
2 Unleavened Bread Keeping apart: distinguishing text, experience, interpretation, confession and application.
3 Feast of Weeks Interpreting: using the four questions, naming source and distortion, and allowing the received Word to form us.
4 Trumpets Awakening: hearing the call and naming a first concrete step.
5 Day of Atonement Formation: responsibility, confession, restoration, habit and community.
6 Tabernacles Dwelling: actually practising the received order, together and provisionally.
7 Closing Day / eighth day Testing in rest: does it hold, point to God and bear fruit?

Shabbat comes first. That matters. We do not begin with an empty battery that our study must recharge. We begin with God’s completed work. In Genesis, the human being awakens in a world God has already made. So our reading too is carried by what He has already given.

The first two stages separate. Just as at Passover the old house is searched and leaven is removed, we learn to distinguish what has become mixed in our reading. Not in order to end with emptiness, but to make room.

Around the Feast of Weeks, the Word comes as gift and calling. There, looking becomes interpretation. We receive not only information; we are formed into a people who hear and respond.

Then follow call, reconciliation and dwelling. A study that stops at insight remains like a building plan on a table. The Word wants to dwell among us: in language, rhythm, relationships, responsibility and hope.

The final stage is rest. It is precisely there that we test. We can fill an ordinary workday with activity, but we cannot force rest. If our interpretation remains standing only because we must continually protect, prove and perform it, perhaps it is not yet a habitable house.

“On the last and greatest day of the feast, Yeshua stood and cried out, ‘If anyone is thirsty, let him come to Me and drink.’”

John 7:37

The last day does not end with our performance, but with thirst and coming. We receive living water. In this way, the seventh stage guards the whole process: the study must not lead us to the method, but to Yeshua. Not to control, but to drinking.

We do not need to move hurriedly between the stages. Sometimes we remain longer at one step. The feasts teach us that God gives time. We sow today and receive fruit later. We count, wait and celebrate. The movement is fixed, but it is carried by grace.

12. The hinge: from looking to building

Apply this:Step 10Step 15

A house that has merely been emptied is not yet a home. A field from which weeds have been removed still needs to be sown. In the same way, every study has a hinge: the moment when we do not only distinguish what is wrong, but begin to receive what God gives in its place.

That hinge lies around the third stage, at the Feast of Weeks. Up to that point, we have mainly looked and kept things apart. Now we apply the four questions. We connect every interpretation to something actually in the text. We name the distortion, but also the good direction.

Scripture knows this double movement. We put off the old self and are renewed. We do not only stop speaking falsehood, but speak truth. We no longer steal, but work so that we can share. God does not merely bring people out of Egypt; He brings them to Himself.

“Put off the old self … and put on the new self.”

Ephesians 4:22–24

Putting off without putting on leaves us naked. Putting on without putting off covers the old without allowing it to die. The hinge keeps both together.

Therefore, with every exposure, we ask: what does the text give in its place? If “do not trust in your own strength” is the warning, the answer is not that we must become powerless. The answer is that we recognise our strength as a gift, use it gratefully and do not forget the Source.

If “do not grasp your calling” is the warning, the alternative is not to wait without responsibility until everything happens by itself. We receive the calling, respond to it and learn to carry it without turning it into a possession.

The hinge prevents our interpretation from becoming merely critical. Criticism can be sharp and still unfruitful. The Spirit exposes in order to renew. He empties in order to fill. He removes our hands from the wheel where we wanted to control God, but He does not leave us without direction. He teaches us to walk behind Yeshua.

13. Do not only dismantle; build something habitable

Apply this:Step 10Step 15

Those who only say what is forbidden often leave the hearer standing in the cold. We can demolish an old house because it is unsafe, but a family cannot remain outside in the rain. The question is therefore always: what true, good and habitable house does the text put in its place?

Not only… But in its place…
“Do not trust in your own strength.” “Receive even your strength as a gift and use it gratefully under God’s authority.”
“Do not be stiff-necked.” “Let your heart be opened so that love for God and for the vulnerable becomes visible.”
“Do not grasp your calling.” “Receive your name, lay aside what covers it and learn to carry it faithfully.”
“Do not turn obedience into merit.” “Truly respond to grace and let obedience become fruit.”

The good replacement is not simply the opposite attitude. Those who reject control do not have to become passive. Those who reject legalism do not have to release the commandment. Those who expose group pressure do not have to end in individualism. The replacement does not come from the swinging pendulum of our reaction, but from God’s Word.

“Therefore put away falsehood and speak truth, each one to his neighbour, for we are members of one another.”

Ephesians 4:25

The text does not only say: do not lie. It puts truth and community in their place. The new behaviour becomes habitable because it is embedded in who we are together.

☐ The replacement is carried by the text itself.

☐ It allows us to remain real human beings, with responsibility and boundaries.

☐ It seeks no scapegoat and does not make one group the cause of everything.

☐ It also changes our language, rhythm and way of relating to others.

☐ It remains correctable and does not need to become untouchable.

☐ An ordinary community can genuinely practise it.

☐ It is not merely the mirror image of the error we oppose.

Habitable also means that grace can breathe there. If the new order works only for strong people who never fail, we may have built a new house of performance. In God’s house there is room for confession, restoration, patience and growth.

Yeshua does not break a bruised reed. He truly exposes, but He also carries. Therefore our application may be clear without becoming harsh. It invites a concrete step, but does not force the fruit.

So we do not build to impress. We build so that people can dwell: receiving, connected, responsible and close to God. And when a plank turns out to be crooked, we repair it. The house is not our monument. It is a place where Yeshua may dwell in our midst.

14. Do you receive it, or seize it?

Apply this:Step 13

This may be the sharpest question in The Frame. It asks not only what we believe, but how we hold truth, calling, identity and grace. Do we receive it from God’s hand, or try to secure it with our own hand?

Receiving is not weak. A child who receives bread must take it, eat it and live by it. Grasping can look very active and devout. It prays, studies, organises and defends. The difference lies not only in the movement of the hands, but in the relationship to the Giver.

Receiving Grasping
The gift comes first; we respond to it. We secure the outcome through technique, pressure or a trick.
Our interpretation remains correctable by the text. The text must prove our interpretation right.
Our interpretation can be corrected. Every counterexample becomes fresh confirmation.
The human being remains a person and a responsible agent. The human being becomes a tool, a mass, a culprit or a piece of evidence.
What is given nearby, we accept. We organise an expedition to fetch what has already been given.
We remain in relationship with the Giver. The Giver becomes a means of retaining the gift.

We can be deeply convinced of something and still allow ourselves to be corrected. That is not weakness. It is trust that truth is stronger than our defence. Healthy conviction has both a backbone and open hands.3

Opposite this stands a way of reasoning in which the starting point has already absorbed every objection in advance. The conclusion can then never truly be tested. The Frame deliberately does not choose such closed certainty.4

Four ways of handling a gift

Around every gift — a word, a calling, grace, our name — four attitudes can arise. The third is the most hidden, because it resembles faithfulness.

Attitude What happens
Despising We reject the gift: “I do not need this.”
Seizing wrongly We take the good with our own hand, before the time or outside the given way.
Sacred clutching We do not want to steal the good, but we clamp it shut so that we cannot lose it. The gift becomes possession.
Receiving We receive from a hand we do not control and respond in trust and obedience.

Sacred clutching is difficult to recognise. We want to preserve the truth, protect our children, remain faithful to our calling or not lose a received experience. That desire can be good. But when we begin to use the Giver to secure the gift, the hand has closed.

Deuteronomy 30 gives another image. The Word is not so far away that we must climb to heaven or cross the sea to fetch it. It is near, in mouth and heart, to do it. Near does not mean that it originates in us. It is given and therefore near.

“This word is very near you, in your mouth and in your heart, so that you may do it.”

Deuteronomy 30:14

We see this movement in Yeshua too. He did not use His sonship to place Himself outside the path of obedience. In the wilderness He refused to turn stones into bread at the tempter’s command. He did not have to prove who He was by turning a gift into a trick.

“Though He was in the form of God, He did not regard equality with God as something to be grasped, but emptied Himself.”

Philippians 2:6–7

That is not a loss of identity. Precisely the one who receives who he is from the Father does not have to fight continually for that identity. He can serve, wait and obey without losing himself.

The core question is therefore not only: are we holding on to something good? But: does it remain a gift? Can we still listen? Can we still hear “no”? Can we place it back in God’s hand without thinking that everything will collapse?

Grace does not empty our hands in order to leave us poor. It empties them so that we can receive again. And receiving does not end in passivity. We respond, carry, serve and abide. Not apart from the vine, but in Yeshua.

15. Dwelling, and the test on the day of rest

Apply this:Step 12Step 13Step 15

When a house seems finished, we sit down inside it. Only then do we hear where the draught comes in, which door sticks and whether the table truly fits. In the same way, we test an interpretation not only on paper, but in rest. Can it be inhabited without our continually forcing it to remain standing?

The final stage therefore has three positive questions. They are the counterpart of all our distinguishing:

  1. Does the interpretation point beyond itself to God, who gives life and truth?

  2. Does it move with the whole of Scripture, or allow only a few texts to speak?

  3. Does it let God speak in His own way, or turn everything into a system we manage?

Then we look at the fruit. Does this interpretation lead to a received identity? To faithfulness, courage and care for the vulnerable? To hope under suffering? To a community in which we can confess and be restored? Or does it produce fear, spiritual pride, dependence on a leader or ever-increasing control?

“You will recognise them by their fruits.”

Matthew 7:16

Fruit is not the same as immediate success. A true word can evoke resistance. Obedience can be costly. But the direction becomes visible: are love, peace, patience, faithfulness and self-control growing? Or does the person become ever smaller beneath a system that never gives rest?

Shabbat helps us hear this question. We stop not because everything is finished, but because God remains God when we stop. The world is not carried by our activity. Neither does our study need to be rescued by our effort.

“The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath.”

Mark 2:27

When even the day of rest becomes a performance by which we prove our purity, we have grasped again. But when we receive rest, we can see what the interpretation does to us when we are not performing it to secure ourselves.

The goal is not a successful diagram. The goal is a person standing before God: receiving, connected, responsible and truly himself or herself. A community that learns to listen. A house in which truth and grace dwell together.

“Come to Me, all who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest.”

Matthew 11:28

That is where the test ends. Not with our assessment, but with His invitation. We may lay down our maps, release our burdens and remain with Yeshua. He is not the final box in the method. He is the rest that carries the whole journey.

PART V — START WORKING

—

A pastoral introduction to the worksheets

The following chapters are practical. They contain steps, times, tables and checking questions. That can help, but it can also become a new temptation. We can even turn attentiveness into a performance and think that a study is good only when every box is filled.

A worksheet cannot listen for us. It can only make space in which we are less likely to forget what we saw. Therefore use a pencil. Leave boxes empty when the text gives no answer. Write “possible” where something is not yet established. Stop when haste takes the place of attention.

The steps are a walking route, not an assembly line. Sometimes we have forty-five minutes; sometimes we carry one question for a whole week. The goal remains the same: not to produce more, but to receive more honestly and respond more faithfully.

“Your words were found, and I ate them; Your word was to me the joy and gladness of my heart.”

Jeremiah 15:16

So we do not fill out a form merely to be finished. We taste the Word. We keep what is given. And where we get stuck, we remain close to Yeshua, who does not judge us by the fullness of our diagram but teaches us to hear His voice.

16. Step by step through the week

Apply this:To the step-by-step plan
Step What we do
1 Record the basic details: parashah, haftarah, place in the year and audience.
2 Read the text twice without commentary. Mark repetition, contrast and questions.
3 Divide it into natural sections and give each one a descriptive title.
4 For each section, write down: who, what, key words, purpose and result.
5 Check the translation, background and fair alternatives.
6 Do the same with the haftarah before we look for a connection.
7 Think of two or three connecting threads and note what each thread leaves open.
8 Apply labels: text, interpretation, confession or application; how certain is it and what may it do?
9 Ask the four questions and connect every interpretation to a place in the text.
10 Name the distorted frame and the good, habitable alternative. Check for a mirror image.
11 Look for the connection with the whole of Scripture and with Yeshua. Give that connection a label too.
12 Develop the call, first step, rhythm and communal formation.
13 Apply the receiving-or-grasping test. Does it point beyond itself, move with Scripture and let God speak?
14 Write the strongest objection and name what we may need to repair.
15 Build one main movement: opening, text, core, application and a landing in grace.
16 Remove forced connections and mark what remains open.

This sequence does not need to receive the same amount of time every week. Sometimes the text is clear and the application needs the greatest care. Sometimes one word is difficult and the rest remains simple. The route helps us omit nothing important, but we continue listening to the passage before us.

At the end, we read our study once more without the notes beside it. Does one movement sound through it? Is the text itself still audible? Does the hearer end up with a burden that must be carried in order to be accepted, or with a gift to which one may genuinely respond?

We do not have to use everything we found. Good preparation often yields more material than the final teaching needs. Deleting can also be receiving. We release what clouds the main movement and perhaps preserve it for later.

17. In forty-five minutes

Apply this:To the step-by-step plan

Sometimes we do not have an entire week. Then a short route can help. Forty-five minutes is no guarantee of depth, but it is enough time not to jump immediately from a striking word to an application.

Time Task
0–10 min Read slowly. Mark five facts, three repetitions and one major contrast.
10–20 min Divide the text into sections and write one peshat sentence for each section.
20–28 min Ask the four questions. Note at least one specific textual reference for each question.
28–34 min Name the old frame, the habitable alternative and the movement of receiving–grasping.
34–40 min Make one connection with the rest of Scripture and Yeshua; give it a label.
40–45 min Write the core sentence, one honest objection and one concrete, gracious application.

When the time is up, we stop. We do not have to fill the gap with a quick discovery. A short study with an honest boundary is more reliable than a full study grasped by haste.

The core sentence may be simple. Not the most impressive sentence, but the sentence that carries the movement of the text. For example: “Human beings forget the Giver when they become full; God does not forget the receiver in emptiness.”

The application too remains small enough to do. Not: change your entire life from today. But: this week, name one received gift before you use it. Or: read the passage again and underline where God Himself acts.

In this way, forty-five minutes does not become a race. It is a bounded space in which we can hear, distinguish and respond. The fruit does not come from the clock, but from abiding with the Word.

18. Common mistakes

Apply this:Step 14Step 16

Mistakes belong to learning how to read. We do not have to hide them. Precisely when we recognise a recurring pattern, we can return to the source. The following mistakes are not a list with which to judge others; they are first a mirror for our own way of working.

Mistake Repair
The message was already fixed before we looked. First write a bare list of what is actually there.
An application is presented as “the meaning.” Say, “this may teach us,” and name the boundary.
A word root, number or letter image determines the sentence. Return to grammar, usage and context.
Every detail is made into a reference to Yeshua. Give the connection a label and preserve the text’s own character.
We only dismantle. Put a text-supported, habitable alternative in its place.
Receiving becomes doing nothing. Preserve the movement: given → answered → carried.
Every objection proves us right. State what could genuinely disprove the interpretation.
The contrast becomes too absolute. Ask whether motives can be mixed and allow nuance.
The application becomes a new checklist. End with God’s initiative, grace and one walkable step.

The most dangerous mistake is sometimes not an incorrect conclusion, but a closed posture. We can use many good words and yet no longer listen. Therefore we apply every test to ourselves as well.

When we discover a mistake, we do not have to discard the whole work. We return to the place where the movement changed. Where did a possibility become a certainty? Where did the gift become a possession? Where did we leave the text behind in order to rescue our message?

There we can repair. We open the hand. We delete a sentence. We name a boundary. We admit that we do not know something. That is not a loss of authority. Authority does not arise because we never correct ourselves, but because we remain under the Word.

PART VI — AN EXAMPLE: EKEV

—

Ekev: the bread we did not know

We take parashah Ekev as our example, especially Deuteronomy 8–10. Alongside it we read Isaiah 49 as the haftarah and propose the temptation of Yeshua in Matthew 4 as a Gospel reading. We do not begin with the connection. We begin with what each text gives in itself.

What we see in the Torah

  • Israel is reminded of the entire journey through the wilderness.

  • Hunger and manna are named together: lack and received bread.

  • The warning not to forget God sounds precisely amid food, houses, flocks and possessions.

  • “My strength” is corrected by: He is the One who gives strength.

  • The land is emphatically not given because of Israel’s own righteousness.

  • Obedience is genuinely required, but it is a response within a given covenant.

The core does not lie only in lack. The danger appears precisely when the cupboard is full. In the wilderness, a person cannot pretend to have made the bread. Every morning the manna lies outside the tent. But in the land, grain grows in fields worked by human hands. Then the gift can easily disguise itself as possession.

“Man does not live by bread alone, but by everything that comes from the mouth of the LORD.”

Deuteronomy 8:3

Bread is not worthless here. God truly feeds. The problem is not eating, building or using strength. The problem arises when the means turns itself into the source. When we say: my strength and the power of my hand produced this wealth for me.

“Remember the LORD your God, for it is He who gives you strength to produce wealth.”

Deuteronomy 8:18

Receiving therefore does not mean that Israel does nothing. The people plough, build and keep. But even the strength with which they work is received. The hand is genuinely active, but it is not the source of its own existence.

What we see in Deuteronomy 9

The next step protects us from an overly simple message. We could summarise Ekev as: everything is a gift, so it does not matter how Israel lives. But Deuteronomy 9 says both that the land is not given because of Israel’s own righteousness and that the people’s stubbornness is serious.

Grace does not erase responsibility. It changes the foundation. Israel does not receive because it has made itself righteous. Precisely for that reason it cannot boast and is genuinely called to listen.

“Not because of your righteousness or the uprightness of your heart are you going in to possess their land.”

Deuteronomy 9:5

What we see in the haftarah

In Isaiah 49, Zion says: the LORD has forsaken me, my Lord has forgotten me. That is the experienced reality. Emptiness speaks as though it has the final word. But God’s answer is not that Zion must try harder to feel remembered.

“Can a woman forget her nursing child? Even if they forget, I will not forget you. See, I have engraved you on the palms of My hands.”

Isaiah 49:15–16

Here we see the other side of the same movement. Human beings can forget the Giver when they become full. God does not forget the receiver when that person feels empty and forsaken. His faithfulness does not rest on our power to remember.

The proposed Gospel reading

In the wilderness, Yeshua is addressed concerning His sonship: if You are the Son of God, turn these stones into bread. The temptation concerns more than hunger. It tries to turn received identity into a means of self-preservation.

Yeshua does not grasp for proof. He does not turn His sonship into a trick. He answers with Deuteronomy 8. He continues to live from the Father’s speech, even when bread is absent.

“Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God.”

Matthew 4:4

In this way, the connection becomes clear without erasing the Torah. Israel had to learn that received bread could not be detached from the Giver. Yeshua remains in that same truth as the obedient Son. He refuses to grasp what He has already received as Son.

The four questions, briefly

Question In Ekev
Where does it come from? Bread, land and strength are given. Human strength is a means and a response, not the source.
Where is it moving? From wilderness to land, from hunger to abundance — and precisely in abundance the danger of forgetting grows.
What is given and what is asked? God gives and reminds; Israel is genuinely called to love, listen and walk faithfully.
Is it truly coherent? Deuteronomy 9 prevents us from separating grace from responsibility; Isaiah 49 prevents us from turning human unfaithfulness into forgetfulness on God’s part.

The distorted frame and the habitable alternative

Distortion Habitable movement
“My strength has produced this.” “Even my strength is received; I use it gratefully and remember the Giver.”
“Because it is grace, obedience does not matter.” “Grace is the soil from which a genuine response grows.”
“My emptiness proves that God has forgotten me.” “My feeling is heard, but God’s faithfulness has the final word.”
“I must prove who I am by doing something extraordinary.” “In Yeshua I receive my identity and do not need to turn it into a trick.”

We can now inhabit this study. For someone who has little, it may mean: my lack is not proof that God is absent. For someone who has much: my full barn is not proof that I am my own source. For all of us: receiving is not doing nothing. We use what has been given, but we continue to remember the Giver.

Core sentence

Human beings forget the Giver when they become full; God does not forget the receiver, even in emptiness.

An honest boundary remains necessary. We must not enlarge the hunger of Deuteronomy 8 into the general statement that every suffering is directly sent by God as a lesson. The text speaks about this particular covenant journey through the wilderness. The application may comfort and warn, but it must preserve that boundary.

The landing does not lie in a technique for never forgetting again. It lies in Yeshua, who remained faithful in the wilderness where Israel and we so easily grasp. We are not saved by perfect remembrance, but by His faithfulness. In Him we learn to receive, respond and abide.

PART VII — WEIGHING AN INTERPRETATION

—

When obedience becomes the foundation

Suppose someone explains a commandment in this way: “By keeping this commandment well, we make ourselves righteous.” That sounds devout and serious. After all, obedience is treated as important. Yet we must ask what place it is given.

The problem is not that the human being responds. The problem is that the response replaces the gift. The fruit is made into the root. What ought to grow from grace becomes the ground on which we think we may stand.

Question What we see in the distortion What preserves the scriptural movement
Where does it come from? Our keeping of the commandment receives creative power. Our interpretation takes the place of the text. God speaks, calls and gives. Obedience arises as a response.
What is given and what is asked? Perform first, receive afterward. The gift comes first; the human being genuinely responds and bears responsibility.
Receiving or grasping? We secure our righteousness through our own effort. We receive righteousness and learn to walk faithfully.
Is it truly coherent? Failure proves “too little was done”; success proves the system. Nothing can contradict it any longer. Grace and commandment may speak together; we name what can correct our interpretation.

The correction must not now end in the mirror image. We do not say: because we are not justified by obedience, obedience does not matter. That would be the same error again, but reversed. Then we would use grace to avoid responding.

“By grace you have been saved, through faith … not by works. For we are His workmanship, created in Yeshua the Messiah to do good works.”

Ephesians 2:8–10

Paul holds both sentences together. Not by works. Created for good works. Works are not the womb of grace; they are its fruit. We do not make ourselves God’s workmanship by living well. Because we are His workmanship, we learn to walk in what He has prepared.

Titus too does not say that grace frees us from formation. Grace trains us. It teaches us to put away ungodliness and to live self-controlled, righteous and devoted lives. But the teacher remains the grace of God, not our fear of losing our place.

“The grace of God has appeared … and it trains us to live self-controlled, righteous and godly lives.”

Titus 2:11–12

That is the habitable house. No obedience as an entrance fee. No grace as an excuse for indifference. But received life that genuinely responds.

Pastorally, this makes a great difference. The person who fails does not have to conclude that he has worked himself outside God’s hand. He may confess, repair and learn to walk again. The person who grows does not have to boast as though he produced himself. He may give thanks.

The final question is therefore not: have we done enough to deserve grace? The question is: do we abide in Yeshua, and does the received life bear fruit?

“Whoever abides in Me and I in him bears much fruit, for apart from Me you can do nothing.”

John 15:5

That is also where this weighing ends. Not with a person who does nothing. Not with a person who saves himself. But with a branch that genuinely bears fruit because it abides in the vine.

C. Short glossary

Term In plain language
Map and land The map is our interpretation; the land is the text itself. The map helps, but must not invent the land.
Peshat The ordinary, first meaning of the text in its own sentences, history and context.
The four questions Where does it come from? Where is it going? What is given and asked? Is it truly coherent?
Receiving and grasping Receiving remains in relationship with the Giver; grasping secures the gift with one’s own hand.
Mirror image We oppose an error so strongly that we end up in the opposite error.

D. Where this comes from

This booklet belongs with the foundational book of The Frame. There the entire line of thought is developed in greater detail: the axes, the stages, the directional test, the relationship between observation and interpretation, and the way we test an interpretation for source, trajectory, will and coherence.

In the main text, only one theologian is deliberately named: E. J. Young. His work offers a clear entry into the confession that Scripture truly is God’s own inspired Word and at the same time was spoken through real human writers. Other witnesses who helped clarify particular parts of the method appear only in the footnotes. They do not carry the authority of the method; they point to conversations in which certain parts became sharper.

The Frame itself does not seek to become a new authority beside Scripture. It is a map to help us listen. Therefore this booklet too must remain correctable. Where the text teaches us differently, the method bows.

We end where we began: God speaks. We receive. We respond. And we abide in Yeshua.
